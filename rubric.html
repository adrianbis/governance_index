The index · version 1.0

Three axes, fifteen indicators

Every framework in the index is scored on the same fifteen indicators, grouped into three axes. Each indicator runs from 0 to 100 against published anchors, so any reader can check a score against the rule that produced it — and disagree with it precisely.

How to read a score

Higher always means more

Within every axis, 100 means more of the thing the axis is named after, and 0 means none of it. A framework scoring 90 on Power Concentration concentrates a great deal of power; one scoring 90 on Entrenchment is very hard to change or leave.

Two axes are named for the property being measured rather than its desirable opposite, which keeps that rule intact. If you would rather read them the other way round, the conversion is subtraction: reversibility = 100 − Entrenchment, and efficiency = 100 − Implementation Friction.

Scores describe, they do not rank

A high score is not a criticism and a low score is not praise. Concentrated authority buys enforceability at the cost of flexibility; loose coordination buys adaptability at the cost of bite. The index makes those trade-offs legible; it does not adjudicate them.

How the axis scores are calculated

An axis score is the unweighted mean of its five indicators, rounded to the nearest whole number. All five indicators count equally. This is a deliberate simplification: we have no defensible basis for weighting one indicator above another, and equal weighting is the assumption a reader is most likely to make anyway.

Values between the anchors

The anchors are reference points, not the only permitted answers. Where a framework sits clearly between two anchors, a coder may assign an intermediate value in steps of 5 — for example 25 for a framework adopted in nine months, which falls between the “under six months” and “one to three years” anchors on adoption lag. Every intermediate value carries a written justification in the coding notes.

One indicator uses four anchors

Enforcement power is scored 0 / 33 / 66 / 100 rather than 0 / 50 / 100, because the step from “peer review” to “financial penalties” to “binding supranational orders” is a genuinely three-step ladder. This does not affect the axis mean, which averages the values themselves rather than the anchor positions.

Axis 1

Power Concentration

How much authority the framework gathers into a single locus, and how compulsory the obligations it creates are. 100 = authority is maximally concentrated and obligations are hard law.

1.1 Centralisation of decision-making

Is there a single entity with final rule-making or enforcement power?

  • 0No central body; authority fully distributed.
  • 50Coordinated but not binding — a secretariat or working group that convenes but cannot decide.
  • 100A single entity with the final say, including through delegated instruments.

1.2 Bindingness

Are the obligations legally compulsory, or voluntary?

  • 0Soft law: voluntary codes, principles, best-practice guidance.
  • 50Mixed — some obligations mandatory, some opt-in.
  • 100Hard law, with legal sanctions for non-compliance.

1.3 Enforcement power

Can the governing body impose fines, suspend rights, or issue binding orders?

  • 0No enforcement mechanism of any kind.
  • 33Peer review or mandatory reporting only.
  • 66Financial penalties possible.
  • 100Full supranational enforcement: fines, suspension of rights, binding orders.

1.4 Exclusivity of agenda-setting renamed

Are non-state actors and smaller states shut out of setting the agenda, or guaranteed a voice in it?

  • 0Broad stakeholder inclusion; civil society holds voting or co-decision rights.
  • 50State-only participation, but with equal representation among states.
  • 100Elite-driven; no meaningful non-state or small-state input.

Originally drafted as “inclusivity of agenda-setting”, marked as an inverse indicator. The anchors are unchanged — only the name, so that the label points the same way as the numbers.

1.5 Supranationality renamed

Is sovereignty pooled, or does each state retain a veto?

  • 0Unanimous intergovernmentalism; a state veto on all matters.
  • 50Qualified majority voting in some areas.
  • 100Full supranational delegation; sovereignty pooled.

Originally “supranational vs. intergovernmental”. Anchors unchanged.

Axis 2

Entrenchment

How hard the framework is to amend, update, exit or challenge once it is in force. 100 = maximally locked in. Read as reversibility, this axis is simply inverted: reversibility = 100 − Entrenchment.

Why this axis was renamed

It was drafted as “Reversibility”, but its five indicators pointed in two directions at once: high amendment rigidity makes a framework less reversible, while strong sunset clauses and open judicial standing make it more so. Averaged together as drafted, the two halves cancelled and the axis score carried almost no information. Three indicators have been inverted and the axis renamed so that all five now count in the same direction. Every original anchor is recorded below its replacement, so nothing has been lost.

2.1 Amendment rigidity

How many veto players must agree before a core rule can be changed?

  • 0Simple majority; amendment is frequent and routine.
  • 50Qualified majority, or bicameral approval required.
  • 100Unanimity, or ratification by every member state.

2.2 Permanence of rules inverted

Do the rules expire or face compulsory reconsideration, or do they simply persist?

  • 0Mandatory periodic review, with automatic expiry if not renewed.
  • 50Ad hoc review possible, but not compulsory.
  • 100No review or sunset provision; rules persist indefinitely.

Originally “sunset / review clauses”, scored 0 = no provisions, 100 = mandatory review with automatic expiry. Inverted so that a framework which is easy to reconsider scores low on entrenchment.

2.3 Rigidity of technical updating inverted

Can technical detail be updated without reopening the whole instrument?

  • 0Robust delegated authority for technical adaptation — delegated acts, comitology, a standing standards body.
  • 50Limited delegation, confined to uncontroversial updates.
  • 100Any change requires full legislative or treaty revision.

Originally “delegation of technical updates”, scored 0 = full revision required, 100 = robust delegation. Inverted for the same reason as 2.2.

2.4 Exit cost renamed

What does it cost a participant to leave — in notice, penalties and consequences?

  • 0No notice period; immediate withdrawal with no consequences.
  • 50Reasonable notice period (around twelve months) with minor penalties.
  • 100Prohibitive exit clauses; severe economic or legal consequences.

Originally “withdrawal ease”. The anchors already ran from easy to prohibitive, so only the name changed — “ease” scored 100 for the hardest exit, which read backwards.

2.5 Insulation from legal challenge inverted

Can individuals or organisations take the framework to court and force a reinterpretation?

  • 0Individuals and organisations have standing before a supranational court.
  • 50State-to-state dispute resolution only.
  • 100No judicial review available; political discretion only.

Originally “justiciability”, scored 0 = no judicial review, 100 = individual standing. Inverted: a framework open to legal challenge is less entrenched, not more.

Axis 3

Implementation Friction

How slow, unsupported and coordination-heavy the framework is to put into practice. 100 = maximum friction. Read as efficiency, the axis is inverted: efficiency = 100 − Implementation Friction.

Why this axis was renamed

It was drafted as “Efficiency”, but every one of its anchors ran from 0 = fast, supported and well-resourced to 100 = slow, unsupported and consensus-bound. An axis called Efficiency on which the least efficient framework scores highest is a reading trap. The anchors are all unchanged; the axis and three of its indicators have been renamed to match the direction the numbers already ran in.

3.1 Adoption lag renamed

How long did the framework take to travel from proposal to entry into force?

  • 0Rapid adoption — under six months from proposal to force.
  • 50Moderate — one to three years.
  • 100Protracted — over five years, or indefinite delay.

Originally “adoption speed”; anchors unchanged. The anchors leave two gaps — six to twelve months, and three to five years — which are coded by interpolation at 25 and 75 respectively.

3.2 Deadline unworkability renamed

Are the compliance deadlines clear, realistic and matched to the capacity of those who must meet them?

  • 0Precise deadlines, adequate transition time, capacity explicitly assessed.
  • 50Defined but tight deadlines, with some flexibility.
  • 100No deadlines at all, or timelines that are unrealistic and unsupported.

Originally “compliance deadlines”; anchors unchanged. Note that the top anchor covers two different failures — the absence of deadlines and the presence of impossible ones. Coders record which applies in the coding notes.

3.3 Implementation support gap renamed

Does the framework arrive with sandboxes, templates, funding and training — or on its own?

  • 0Full support package: regulatory sandboxes, templates, funding, training.
  • 50Partial support — guidance published, but no funding.
  • 100Rules imposed with no implementation assistance whatsoever.

Originally “implementation support”; anchors unchanged.

3.4 Coordination cost

How many actors must agree at each stage of implementation?

  • 0A single implementing body; no veto players.
  • 50Multiple actors, but a clear hierarchy among them.
  • 100Full consensus required at every stage of implementation.

3.5 Adaptive rigidity renamed

In practice, does the framework keep pace with technical change, or does every change require reopening it?

  • 0Adaptation built in; dynamic standards that move with the technology.
  • 50Periodic updates possible, but each requires a formal process.
  • 100Static; any change requires full re-legislation.

Originally “adaptive efficiency”; anchors unchanged. This indicator deliberately overlaps with 2.3, rigidity of technical updating: 2.3 asks whether the legal authority to update without reopening the instrument exists, while 3.5 asks whether updating demonstrably happens. See the limitation noted below.

Known limitations

Two indicators measure closely related things

2.3 and 3.5 both concern how easily a framework can be updated. We have kept both because they capture different objects — legal capacity versus observed practice — but a framework with no delegated updating authority will tend to score high on both, which inflates the correlation between Entrenchment and Implementation Friction. Readers comparing those two axes should treat the relationship as partly built in rather than discovered.

Some rows are families of instruments, not single instruments

The United Nations and African Union rows each cover more than one instrument, and those instruments differ sharply on exactly the properties the index measures — the Malabo Convention is a binding treaty, the Continental AI Strategy is not. Where a row bundles instruments, we score the regime as a whole as it currently operates, and record in the coding notes which instrument drove each score. This is the weakest point in the current design and the most likely thing to change in version 2.

The scores are expert judgements, not measurements

Every score is assigned by the three authors reading primary documents against the anchors above. We have not yet run inter-coder reliability checks, and no framework has been scored blind. The anchors exist so that a reader who disagrees can say exactly which anchor we applied wrongly.

Equal weighting is an assumption, not a finding

Averaging five indicators equally implies each contributes equally to the axis. Nothing in the underlying literature establishes that. We use equal weights because they are transparent and because any alternative would need a justification we cannot yet give.
