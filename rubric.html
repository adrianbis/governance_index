The index · version 2.0

Three axes, twelve features

Every framework in the index is coded on the same twelve features, four to an axis, weighted equally. Each runs from 0 to 100 against published anchors, so any reader can check a score against the rule that produced it — and disagree with it precisely.

How to read a score

Higher always means more

Within every axis, 100 means more of the thing the axis is named after. A framework scoring 90 on power concentration concentrates a great deal of authority; one scoring 90 on reversibility is easy to amend, exit or let lapse.

Two features are reverse-coded

Two features on the reversibility axis are named for the property that reduces reversibility. Their raw scores are recorded as named, then inverted before entering the axis mean, so that all four features count in the same direction.

The inversion, stated plainly

2.2 Amendment difficulty and 2.4 Path dependency enter the reversibility mean as 100 minus their raw score. A framework that is hard to amend and strongly self-reinforcing is less reversible, and the arithmetic has to say so. Raw scores are shown on the scores page alongside the value that actually enters the mean.

Scores describe, they do not rank

A high score is not a criticism and a low score is not praise. Concentrated authority buys enforceability at the cost of flexibility; loose coordination buys adaptability at the cost of bite. The index makes those trade-offs legible; it does not adjudicate them.

How the axis scores are calculated

An axis score is the unweighted mean of its four features, rounded to the nearest whole number, with the two reverse-coded features inverted first. All four features count equally. This is a deliberate simplification: we have no defensible basis for weighting one feature above another, and equal weighting is the assumption a reader is most likely to make anyway.

Values between the anchors

The anchors are reference points, not the only permitted answers. Where a framework sits clearly between two anchors, we assign an intermediate value in steps of 5 and record why. Intermediate values are common — international frameworks rarely sit exactly on an anchor.

Every score carries its reasoning

Each coded cell has a one-line justification and a citation to a primary source. This follows from the standard we hold ourselves to: Lincoln and Guba establish dependability and confirmability through exactly such an audit trail. A score without a recorded justification would fail our own criterion.

The four coding rules

Anchors alone do not make a coding reproducible. These four rules decide what is being scored before anyone asks how well it scores, and they bind every framework in the sample.

Rule 1 — Score the instrument, not its reception

Code what the text provides on its face. What happened afterwards — enforcement records, lobbying, political resistance, actual compliance, real-world adoption — is out of scope. Those measure the environment a framework landed in rather than the framework's design, and they introduce more variables than an index of this size can carry. Where the text creates a power, the power counts even if it has never been used.

Rule 2 — Code the framework as adopted

The object of coding is the instrument at adoption, not as subsequently amended. Later amendments are recorded and discussed, but they do not move a score. This keeps the sample comparable: several frameworks here have never been amended, and coding one against a consolidated later text while the others stand at adoption would compare different things.

What this means for the reader

The index is a snapshot of design choices at the moment of commitment. It does not claim to describe what any framework looks like today. The EU AI Act, for one, was substantively amended in July 2026; that amendment is recorded on the scores page and excluded from the scores.

Rule 3 — Where a row covers several instruments, score the dominant one

Pick the instrument that carries the operative obligations — the one that will end up being applied — name it, and code that at face value. The others are context, not inputs to the score.

Rule 4 — Resource and technical concentration measure concentration among the parties

Features 1.3 and 1.4 ask how unequally material and technical capability are distributed among the parties to the framework — not how much leverage the framework's sponsor holds over outsiders. This follows the middle-power and great-power bargaining literature the two features are drawn from, and it keeps the axis coherent: all four features then answer the same question, which is how power is distributed inside the arrangement.

What this rule costs

The index cannot record the Brussels effect — the capacity to set global rules through market access rather than through membership. That omission sits alongside the absence of Lukes' third face, and both concern power exercised over those outside the room. They are the strongest candidates for what a version 3 should add.

Axis 1

Power concentration

How much authority the framework gathers into a single locus. 100 = authority maximally concentrated. The first two features follow the faces of power: who gets to vote, and who decides what is discussed in the first place. The second two follow the material and technical resources on which parties bargain.

1.1 Decision-making concentration

Who gets to vote, and can the outcome bind those who voted against it?

  • 0All parties hold an equal vote and an effective veto; no weighting, no delegation of decision rights.
  • 50Weighted or majority voting among states; no single actor can decide alone.
  • 100A single body takes binding decisions that bind non-consenting parties.

1.2 Agenda-setting concentration

Who decides what reaches the table in the first place?

  • 0Any party, including non-state actors, can place items on the agenda; open co-decision on scope.
  • 50Agenda set collectively by member states, or by a rotating or shared presidency.
  • 100A single actor holds a formal or de facto monopoly on initiating proposals.

1.3 Resource concentration

Does material weight — economy, population, market access — convert into formal influence?

  • 0No material asymmetry converts into influence; formal equality is also practical equality.
  • 50Larger economies carry informal weight, but the formal rules do not encode it.
  • 100Material weight is formally encoded — population- or GDP-weighted voting, budget control, or contribution-proportional rights.

1.4 Technical concentration

Who holds the know-how that determines what compliance actually means?

  • 0Technical content set through open participation by the parties; expertise widely distributed among them.
  • 50Technical work sits with a designated body, but participation is genuinely open and reviewable.
  • 100Technical content delegated to a narrow expert body outside the parties' collective control.
Axis 2

Reversibility

How easily a commitment can be undone — left, amended, allowed to lapse, or escaped from. 100 = maximally reversible. The axis follows constitutional design literature, in which institutional flexibility is preferred to irreversible commitment.

2.1 Exit possibility

Can a member state leave, and at what cost?

  • 0No exit short of leaving a wider union; no withdrawal provision at all.
  • 50Withdrawal provided for, with a notice period and moderate consequences.
  • 100Participation voluntary throughout; a party may cease complying at any time with no consequence.

2.2 Amendment difficulty reverse-coded

How many veto players must agree before a core rule can be changed?

  • 0Amendable by a single actor or simple majority; amendment is routine.
  • 50Requires a defined multi-actor legislative procedure short of unanimity.
  • 100Requires unanimity or ratification by every party.

A high score here means less reversibility, so the raw score is inverted before it enters the axis mean: it contributes 100 − raw. Both values are shown on the scores page.

2.3 Sunset mechanisms

Do commitments face compulsory review, or expire if not renewed?

  • 0No review or expiry provision; commitments persist indefinitely.
  • 50Mandatory periodic review with reporting, but no expiry — review alone cannot end the commitment.
  • 100Commitments expire automatically unless actively renewed.

2.4 Path dependency reverse-coded

Is the framework self-reinforcing — does staying in get cheaper, and leaving dearer, over time?

  • 0The instrument creates no institutions and requires no investment; ceasing to follow it strands nothing.
  • 50Sunk compliance investment by regulated parties, but no dedicated institutions established by the instrument.
  • 100The instrument establishes dedicated institutions, a certification or registration infrastructure, and continuing obligations, so that reversal would strand investment by states, institutions and regulated entities alike.

A high score here means less reversibility, so the raw score is inverted before it enters the axis mean: it contributes 100 − raw.

Axis 3

Efficiency

How well the framework turns decisions into implementation. 100 = maximally efficient. Efficiency is hard to sustain in global frameworks, which is precisely why it tends to be bought by concentrating power rather than by distributing deliberation.

3.1 Deliberation speed

How long do commitment procedures take, from proposal to entry into force?

  • 0Over five years, or indefinite.
  • 50One to three years.
  • 100Under one year.

Interpolate at 25 for three to five years. The clock stops at entry into force; the gap between force and obligations actually applying is captured under 3.2, so the two are not double-counted. Per rule 1, delays caused by lobbying or political resistance are not modelled.

3.2 Binding implementation

Do members actually have to apply it, uniformly, on the date it says?

  • 0No obligation to apply; adoption entirely discretionary.
  • 50Obligation exists but requires national transposition, or is subject to broad discretion or deferral.
  • 100Directly applicable and uniformly binding, with obligations that take effect on dates fixed in the text.

3.3 Optimised coordination

Does it duplicate or contradict law the parties already have?

  • 0Substantial duplication or contradiction with existing law, unaddressed in the text.
  • 50Overlaps identified and partly resolved on the face of the instrument, with residual duplication.
  • 100Cleanly demarcated from adjacent regimes; no duplicated obligations.

3.4 Compliance verification

Is compliance monitored over the long term, and does anything follow from failing?

  • 0No monitoring of any kind.
  • 50Self-reporting or peer review, without independent verification or consequence.
  • 100Independent third-party verification, with registries, incident reporting and enforceable penalties.

Known limitations

The index describes design at adoption, not governance today

This follows directly from rule 2, and it is the limitation most likely to be mistaken for an error. A framework that has since been amended, deferred or quietly ignored still scores as it was written on the day it was agreed. That is a defensible scope — it is what makes the five frameworks comparable — but it is a scope, not a claim about the present.

Design is not outcome

Rule 1 keeps reception out of the scores, which means the index cannot distinguish a framework whose enforcement powers are used from one whose identical powers sit unused. Two frameworks can score alike and behave nothing alike. The index measures the instrument that was built, not the governance that followed.

No inter-coder reliability testing

Nothing has been coded blind, and nothing has been coded twice. With twelve features and five frameworks, double-coding even two frameworks would materially strengthen the dependability claim the project makes for itself.

Lukes' third face is not measured

The power axis captures decision-making and agenda-setting. It does not capture the third dimension — the shaping of what parties come to want. In AI governance that dimension is substantial: norm diffusion and third-country adoption of regulatory templates are exactly this kind of power, and the index currently has nowhere to record them.

The scores are expert judgements, not measurements

Every score is assigned by reading primary documents against the anchors above. We have not yet run inter-coder reliability checks, and no framework has been coded blind. The anchors exist so that a reader who disagrees can say exactly which anchor we applied wrongly.

Version history

Version 2.0 · September 2026

Index restructured from fifteen indicators to twelve features, four per axis. Power concentration rebuilt around the faces of power plus material and technical resources. Reversibility and efficiency restored as axis names, with 2.2 and 2.4 reverse-coded so that all four features in the reversibility axis count in the same direction.

The four coding rules were settled on 21 September 2026 and the anchors for 1.3, 1.4, 2.4, 3.2, 3.3 and 3.4 were tightened to match them — most substantially 2.4, whose top anchor had included a condition about adoption by non-parties that rule 1 excludes, and which would otherwise have been unreachable by construction.
